https://doi.org/10.1017/elo.2026.10105
Abstract (original): Abstract In this contribution, the author develops a feminist account of an EU Law of the everyday by examining how European migration and labour law structurally produce the invisibility of migrant women. Drawing on the story of Nazneen, an Afghan migrant featured in Ben Judah’s This is Europe, the author argues that her experiences of restricted mobility, informal labour, and gender-based vulnerability are not marginal to the law but shaped by it. The analysis shows how legal regimes, particularly the Common European Asylum System and labour markets structures, generate “everyday borders” and sustain precarious forms of work on which sectors of the European economy depend. Such a reorientation exposes the extent to which European legal and economic orders rely on, and reproduce, the invisibility of vulnerable individuals.

Abstract (original): Yuen v Li is the first English decision to consider the legal treatment of digital assets since the Property (Digital Assets etc) Act 2025 was enacted. The case shows that recognising digital assets as possible ‘third things’ does little to settle what legal consequences follow. In particular, it leaves unresolved whether: (i) interference with digital assets should constitute conversion, a conversion‐like tort, or should attract some other form of liability; (ii) whether such liability should be strict or fault‐based; (iii) how such liability can be reconciled with the rights of those who own the physical devices and ledgers through which digital assets are accessed or maintained; and (iv) how damages should be assessed in volatile and highly manipulated crypto‐markets. 13. Post‐Breach Events, Itemisation and the Avoided Loss Rule
Hadfield, M. (2026). Post‐Breach Events, Itemisation and the Avoided Loss Rule. Mod Law Rev.
https://doi.org/10.1111/1468-2230.70074
Abstract (original): In English law, the following equation is used to compute damages awards: (a) loss sustained in consequence of the breach of duty (‘prima facie loss’) minus (b) benefits obtained as a result of the breach of duty = (c) net loss. This paper explores the manner in which prima facie loss is defined. It argues that prima facie losses are itemised; the description of a particular loss includes within it a reference to the transaction from which that loss arose and to the character of the loss. Further, it demonstrates that whilst a causal connection test is used to determine which gains (on the ‘b’ side of the net loss equation) are to be offset against prima facie losses on the ‘a’ side, a post‐breach event which diminishes or eradicates a claimant's prima facie loss will be taken into account irrespective of any causal connection between that event and the defendant's breach of duty (or the claimant's mitigating steps). The import of this is that some of the avoided loss case law simply does not involve an application of the causal connection test and those cases are therefore irrelevant to an understanding of the normative principles underlying that test.

17. The Mind Does Not Make Jumps
Pallagrosi, J. (2026). The Mind Does Not Make Jumps. European Journal of Philosophy.
https://doi.org/10.1111/ejop.70127
Abstract (original): Abstract Bertrand Russell's notion of acquaintance—still influential in contemporary philosophy of mind and epistemology—promises an immediate and epistemically secure awareness of things, independent of conceptualization, predication, and other forms of cognitive activity. This paper reconstructs and assesses the largely neglected critique of that model developed by George Dawes Hicks (1862–1941). Hicks articulates a distinctive and underexplored source of a line of criticism that later became central to attacks on the Given. He argues that wholly passive and receptive awareness cannot serve as the basis of cognition. If discrimination, comparison, and the apprehension of relations are assigned to a subsequent, heterogeneous faculty, their emergence becomes psychologically unintelligible: such a view would supply no bridge from mere presentation to cognition. I show that this objection rests on a distinctive “reverse engineering” methodology, according to which basic mental phenomena are theoretical posits whose legitimacy depends, at least in part, on their capacity to explain mature cognition. I reconstruct Hicks's master argument and test it against an alternative framework developed from James Ward's theory of attention.

Abstract (original): Abstract Our sensory environments are attentionally landscaped: they have been changed in order to affect our patterns of attention. Abstract (original): Abstract Niels Bohr is standardly read as an instrumentalist or a cautious anti-realist.
